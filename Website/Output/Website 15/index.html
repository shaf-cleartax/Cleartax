Principles

  • Restraint over decoration. If a layout starts looking like a rainbow, we've gone too far.
  • One job per colour. Violet means "click me". Lime means "look here". Nothing else competes.
  • Space is a material. Off-white covers half of any layout on purpose.
FoundationsLogo

Logo

Our wordmark is the clearest signal of who we are. Keep it clean, keep it legible, and give it room to breathe.

Download logo files↓

Lockups

Two lockups cover every case — white on dark, navy on light. There is no third variant.

Clear space and sizing

Clear space

Keep clear space equal to the height of the wordmark on all sides. Never let other elements crowd it.

Minimum size

Never scale below 80px wide on screen, or 20mm wide in print — legibility comes first.

Usage

Do

  • Use the white mark on navy or dark photography
  • Use the navy mark on off-white or light backgrounds
  • Scale the logo proportionally

Don't

  • Stretch, skew, or rotate the wordmark
  • Recolour it outside navy or white
  • Place it on a busy or low-contrast background

FoundationsTypography

Typography

Inter Tight carries display and heading moments. Google Sans handles paragraph, overline, and label copy. Weights map Medium = 500, Semibold = 600, Bold = 700, and no named style carries letter-spacing except uppercase overlines.

Download typography files↓

Every
character counts

Type is where a compliance product either earns trust or quietly loses it. Our screens carry invoice tables, GSTINs, filing deadlines and reconciliation reports, and every one of those has to be read correctly the first time — so we’re introducing two new typefaces to the brand. They need to be well-crafted, hard-working, and legible at sizes most brands never have to think about; ours goes down to 10px in a label.

But legibility on its own isn’t a voice. We also need type that sounds like us: direct, certain, unhurried. The result is two typefaces that divide the work. Inter Tight takes every display and heading moment — its narrower letterforms give long compliance terms room to sit on one line, and carry authority without raising their voice. Google Sans takes paragraph, label and overline copy — a taller x-height keeps mixed-case and lowercase text clear all the way down to 10px, so numbers still read inside a dense table.

Inter Tight speaks. Google Sans explains.

Best practices

Our typography guidelines ensure consistency, readability, and a clear visual hierarchy across all designs. Ported from Typography Best Practices in Figma — and like the Figma frame, each sample below commits the mistake it warns about, so the specimen is the explanation.

✕ Do not make the spacing between lines too tight or too loose
✕ Do not use heavy font weights for headlines
✕ Do not use multiple font weights in headlines
✕ Do not introduce any new typefaces
✕ Do not add iconsor images between text

The positive form of all five: pick one weight per headline, keep line-height on the ramp above, stay inside Inter Tight and Google Sans, and let icons sit beside a text block rather than inside a sentence.

Do's and don'ts

The set only reads as a set because every icon obeys the same three constraints: one line weight, one accent colour, one white chip. Break any of them and the icon stops looking like it came from here.

Do

  • Keep the white chip intact — it's what lets the set sit on off-white without disappearing
  • Scale the whole icon, chip included, so the line weight stays proportional
  • Pick the icon whose literal meaning matches; a close-enough metaphor confuses more than no icon
  • Use one icon per meaning across the product, and reuse it everywhere that meaning appears

Don't

  • Introduce a second colour inside a single icon
  • Place an icon directly on a coloured background — the white chip needs a light surface
  • Crop the chip away to a bare glyph, or recolour the chip
  • Redraw, rotate, or mirror an icon to make a new one — request it instead
  • Mix these with icons from another library in the same view

4 icons are unnamed in the source. Group 1814222686, Group 1814222716, Group 1814222721, and Group 1814227111 are raw Figma layer IDs. They still download correctly, but they can't be searched by meaning until they're renamed upstream.

FoundationsSpacing

Spacing

Every gap, pad, and margin comes off one 17-step scale. Consistent spacing is what makes a layout feel deliberate rather than assembled — it does more for rhythm than any single colour or type choice.

The scale

Drawn to actual size. The scale is 4px-based, with a 2px half-step at the bottom for hairline gaps. The full 17-token table with variable names lives on Design tokens → Spacing.

2 4 8 12 16 24 32 40 48 56 64

Above 64px the scale continues at 72, 80, 88, 96, 104, and 112 — those are section-level gaps, not component-level ones.

Do's and don'ts

Do

  • Take every value from the scale — 8, 12, 16, never 10 or 18
  • Use tighter spacing inside a component than around it, so grouping is legible
  • Step up the scale for bigger jumps (16 → 24 → 32), don't nudge by 2
  • Let one spacing value repeat down a page; repetition reads as rhythm

Don't

  • Invent an off-scale value to make something "just fit"
  • Space a label further from its field than from the next field
  • Use the same gap for every relationship — hierarchy needs contrast
  • Fix a crowding problem with a font-size change instead of spacing

On the scale vs off it

Both cards below hold identical content. The left one uses 8 / 4 / 16 from the scale; the right one uses 7 / 13 / 21. The right isn't obviously broken — that's the point. It just never quite settles.

Shadows & blurs

Use shadows and blurs as style effects to add depth and realism to designs. Ported from the Shadows & Blurs page in Figma — values below are the literal effect tokens. Click any token name to copy it.

Shadows

Shadows serve a number of purposes from aesthetics to functionality. They can enable you to signify that an element is interactive, like making an element seem clickable.

Every shadow is cast by #101828 at low opacity and offset straight down — never tinted, never angled. Larger tokens also pull their spread inward (the negative third value) so the shadow stays under the element instead of haloing it.

Blurs

Blurs can convey the idea of inactive or inaccessible elements, like thumbnails for unlocked game levels.

These are background blurs — in Figma BACKGROUND_BLUR, in CSS backdrop-filter. They blur whatever sits behind the layer, which is what makes them right for overlays, sheets, and disabled surfaces. To blur the element's own pixels instead, that's a layer blur, and it isn't in the token set.

Do's and don'ts

Depth is a hierarchy signal, not decoration. The further an element sits from the page, the more it should be asking for attention — and only one thing can be asking at a time.

Do

  • Map the six shadows to real elevation: xsmall/small for resting cards, medium/large for menus and popovers, xlarge/xxlarge for modals
  • Raise a card's shadow one step on hover to signal it's clickable
  • Pair a blur with a translucent fill, so text on top stays readable
  • Use blur to push a surface back — sheets, disabled tiles, anything behind an overlay

Don't

  • Invent a shadow — no custom offsets, no tinted or coloured casts, no upward angles
  • Put xlarge or xxlarge on something that isn't floating above the whole page
  • Stack a shadow and a border to fake more depth; pick one edge treatment
  • Blur behind body copy the user has to read — reach for a solid surface instead

If two elements on screen carry the same shadow, they're claiming the same layer — which is fine when they genuinely are peers (a row of cards) and a bug when they aren't (a card and the modal above it).

What to look for

  • Off-white carries the layout. Roughly half of every screen is empty space — that's deliberate, not unfinished.
  • Violet only ever means "act". Track it through the flow: it appears on the primary action and nowhere else.
  • Lime punctuates, never decorates. One highlight per screen at most.
  • Navy does the talking. Headlines and body copy sit in navy on off-white, not pure black on white.
  • Icons keep their white chip. Same circle, same single-colour line, at every size.

GuidelinesMotion

Motion

Motion confirms an action happened and orients the eye during a change of state — it never exists for its own sake. These are the exact durations and easings this site's own transitions run on.

Duration

Nav accordion
260ms
View fade-in
300ms
Block stagger step
30ms
Lightbox open
280ms
Lightbox close
160ms
Cursor tracking
120ms

Under 150ms reads as instant feedback (hover snaps, the cursor). 200–300ms suits a full view or panel change. Closing is always faster than opening — leaving should feel quicker than arriving.

Easing

power2.out
Used on every entrance — view fade-in, nav accordion open, lightbox open. Fast start, gentle settle.
power1.in
Used only on the lightbox close — starts slow, accelerates away, signalling "leaving."
power3.out
Used for cursor tracking only — the snappiest curve, reserved for something that follows the pointer every frame.

When to animate

Do

  • Confirm a view or state change (route switch, accordion, lightbox)
  • Soften an abrupt layout shift instead of a hard cut
  • Stagger a short list of blocks (12 or fewer) so it reads as one arrival

Don't

  • Animate on every hover — reserve it for clickable elements
  • Stagger a long list; past ~12 items it reads as lag, not polish
  • Chain more than two motions for one action

Every transition here is skipped outright under prefers-reduced-motion — views still switch, the accordion still opens, just without the tween.

Violet on off-white passes AA for normal and large text but not AAA — safe for headlines, links, and icons; for dense small body copy use Navy or --muted-strong instead. --muted (#6B6878) sits at 4.80:1 on off-white — fine for large or secondary text, too low to trust for small body copy at AAA.

Focus & interaction

Every interactive element shows a visible focus state on keyboard navigation — a 2px violet outline via :focus-visible, never suppressed. The custom cursor is decorative only: it's skipped entirely on touch, coarse pointers, and under prefers-reduced-motion, so no interaction ever depends on it.

Alt text

Icon tiles carry an empty alt="" with the visible name moved into a hover/focus tooltip (.icon-tip) and an aria-label on the button — so a screen reader gets one clear name instead of reading the image twice. The four unnamed "Group…" icons flagged on the Icons page get their raw Figma ID as that name until they're renamed upstream, rather than an empty label that would read as nothing at all.

GuidelinesVoice & content

Voice & content

This page's own copy is the spec — short, declarative sentences that state a rule and then the reason for it. Every doc-lead on this site follows the same shape; here's why, made explicit.

Principles

  • State the rule, then the reason. "Off-white covers half of any layout on purpose" — not "we recommend generous use of white space for a clean aesthetic."
  • One sentence, one idea. If a lead paragraph needs a semicolon to fit everything in, it's two sentences that haven't been split yet.
  • Name the next step, not the mechanism. Buttons say "Book a demo" and "Talk to an expert" — never "Submit" or "Learn more about our platform."
  • Flag gaps instead of hiding them. See Design tokens → Known gaps — say what's missing and why, rather than smoothing it over.

In practice

Avoid

"Oops! Something went wrong. Please try again later."

Prefer

"The portal didn't accept this return. Retry now, or save it as a draft and file before the 20th."

Avoid

"Our platform leverages automation to seamlessly revolutionize your filing experience."

Prefer

"Restraint over decoration. If a layout starts looking like a rainbow, we've gone too far." — the Overview page, unchanged.
